\begin{abstract}
Every existing RGB-thermal fusion benchmark (LLVIP, M3FD, KAIST) is built
on co-located or heavily calibrated rigs, so its evaluation protocol
silently assumes what classical multi-view learning theory
\citep{blum1998cotraining} requires: that the two modalities are exact,
registered views of the same instance. Real dual-optic sensor rigs violate
this, and every existing empirical fix reports the error of \emph{one
trained model} -- confounded by architecture, training budget, and
optimization luck. We instead introduce a \textbf{classifier-agnostic
Bayes-risk benchmark}: closed-form Bhattacharyya bounds
\citep{bhattacharyya1943,fukunaga1990} on the \emph{optimal} classifier's
error, computed directly from class-conditional feature distributions with
no gradient descent anywhere, cross-checked against a nonparametric
kernel-density estimate and validated on synthetic data with known
separation before being trusted on real patches. Applied to 35,952 real
bird/drone instances from \datasetname{} (Aerial Visible-Infrared Drone
dataset, a large-scale RGB-IR video dataset for counter-drone
surveillance), this reveals a fusion advantage 3-4$\times$ larger than any
trained model showed us (Bayes bound at the corpus's modal 12-16px scale:
RGB=0.148, IR=0.198, \textbf{fusion=0.046}); an asymmetric corruption
ceiling (RGB's bound roughly triples under noise-type corruptions, IR's
barely moves); and a new \textbf{Bayes Fusion Gain} metric that shrinks
16\% (0.111$\to$0.093) as injected cross-modal misalignment reaches the
real measured ceiling of this corpus's own misalignment (17px, from a
real 300-pair sample where 32.3\% of pairs exceed 5px against targets that
are themselves only 8-16px wide) -- instantiated alongside a numerical
data-processing-inequality argument showing real mutual information
between the fused representation and the label degrading by 9\% over the
same range. We also introduce the \textbf{Alignment Robustness Score
(ARS)}, a complementary, architecture-agnostic metric for a single trained
model's empirical sensitivity to misalignment (structurally analogous to
certified adversarial robustness \citep{cohen2019certified}), and validate
it on real YOLOv8n baselines: fusion beats both single modalities (0.685
vs. 0.570 RGB-only / 0.504 IR-only, matched mAP50), with a measured
critical radius of 15px -- consistent with, and now explained by, the
Bayes-risk framework's own findings. Beyond these two benchmarks, we
release: 28,790 training / 3,250 validation RGB-IR pairs across 115 videos
(after identifying and removing a systematic noise-augmented duplicate the
source export had silently baked into every frame); \corruptname, a
reproducible ImageNet-C-style corruption suite (7 families $\times$ 5
severities); a measured split-leakage pitfall (naive frame-level splitting
leaks 87.5\% of "training" frames vs. 0.0\% for the video-level splitting
we use); and a documented, already-executed cross-modal translation
baseline (CycleGAN-Turbo). We also disclose a real data-organization
failure found during release preparation -- the source Test split's RGB
and IR files use non-overlapping numbering schemes with no manifest to
resolve them -- as a limitation rather than working around it silently.
Code (including everything that produced every number above) and the
dataset (CC-BY-NC-4.0, Hugging Face) are both released alongside this
paper.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Counter-drone (anti-UAV) vision systems must detect, classify, and track
small aerial objects that are adversarial to exactly the sensing modalities
that are cheapest to deploy. RGB cameras fail in darkness, fog, and glare,
and lose signal entirely once a drone is a few dozen pixels wide at range.
Thermal (IR) cameras keep working in the dark and see through some
atmospheric scattering, but lack the spatial and textural detail needed to
distinguish a drone from a bird, or to read its silhouette at all once its
thermal signature is weak (e.g. a coasting, low-power drone against a warm
background). The natural response -- fusing RGB and IR -- is well
established for pedestrian and vehicle detection (KAIST
\citep{hwang2015kaist}, LLVIP \citep{jia2021llvip}, M3FD
\citep{liu2022tardal}, RoadScene \citep{xu2020fusiondn}), but the public
data situation for \emph{drones as the fused target} is structurally
thinner than it looks at first glance.

\paragraph{The gap.} We surveyed the two adjacent lines of public RGB-T and
drone-vision datasets (Table~\ref{tab:related-work}, Figure
\ref{fig:landscape}) and found no dataset that is simultaneously (a) RGB-IR
\emph{paired}, (b) has a \emph{drone} (not a pedestrian or vehicle) as the
target class, and (c) ships video/tracking data rather than isolated still
pairs. The RGB-T pair datasets closest in spirit to fusion research
(LLVIP, M3FD, RoadScene, KAIST, FLIR ADAS) are all pedestrian/vehicle
detection corpora with no drones at all. The datasets built specifically
for anti-drone vision (Anti-UAV \citep{jiang2023antiuav}, Anti-UAV410
\citep{huang2023antiuav410}, the 3rd Anti-UAV Challenge
\citep{huang20233rdantiuav}, CST Anti-UAV \citep{xie2025cstantiuav}) are
thermal-\emph{only}: there is no paired RGB stream to fuse against. And the
datasets that do pair a drone-mounted camera with thermal imagery
(DroneRGBT \citep{peng2020dronergbt}, VTUAV \citep{zhang2022vtuav}) or with
RGB alone (VisDrone \citep{zhu2022visdrone}) have the camera on the drone,
looking down at pedestrians or generic scenes -- the inverse configuration
from anti-drone surveillance, where the camera is on the ground looking up
or out at the drone.

\paragraph{A gap in how fusion is evaluated, not just in what data exists.}
The deeper problem is not merely that no dataset combines these three
properties -- it is that the datasets which come closest (LLVIP, M3FD,
KAIST) are built on co-located or heavily calibrated rigs, so their
evaluation protocols implicitly assume the two modalities are exact,
registered views of the same instance. This is precisely the assumption
classical multi-view learning theory \citep{blum1998cotraining} requires
and real dual-optic sensor rigs violate. No existing RGB-T benchmark's
evaluation protocol can even detect this violation, let alone measure how
much fusion depends on it -- and every existing empirical attempt to
measure it reports the error of \emph{one trained model}, confounded by
architecture, training budget, and optimization luck. We introduce two
complementary tools that can. The primary one requires no model training
at all: a \textbf{classifier-agnostic Bayes-risk benchmark}
(\S\ref{sec:bayes}) built on closed-form Bhattacharyya bounds
\citep{bhattacharyya1943,fukunaga1990} on the \emph{optimal} classifier's
error, computed directly from real patches, which yields a new
\textbf{Bayes Fusion Gain} metric and a numerical instantiation of the
data-processing inequality \citep{covertom2006} against real measured
misalignment. The second, complementary tool measures a single trained
model's own empirical sensitivity: the \textbf{Alignment Robustness Score
(ARS)} (\S\ref{sec:ars}), which reports a fusion model's accuracy as a
function of injected cross-modal misalignment, summarized as a normalized
area-under-curve and a certified-radius-style critical threshold --
directly analogous to certified adversarial robustness
\citep{cohen2019certified}, but for a geometric perturbation between
modalities rather than an adversarial pixel perturbation to one input.

\paragraph{What we release.} \datasetname{} is both a dataset in its own
right and the testbed that makes both benchmarks necessary rather than
academic:
RGB-IR paired video and frame-pair data with drones and birds as the
target classes, captured under realistic ground-based surveillance
conditions (hilly regions, forest cover, cloudy sky, fog/mist), with RGB
and IR sensors that are only \emph{weakly} aligned because that is what a
real dual-optic gimbal actually delivers. Rather than pretend the pairs
are pixel-registered (as LLVIP/M3FD/KAIST's rigs allow), we quantify the
residual geometric offset per pair: on a real 300-pair sample, 32.3\% of
pairs exceed 5px of measured misalignment (\S\ref{sec:alignment}) against
targets that are themselves only 8-16px wide (\S\ref{sec:small-object}) --
large enough, relative to the target, that it is not a rounding error a
fusion model can safely ignore.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{A classifier-agnostic Bayes-risk benchmark}
    (\S\ref{sec:bayes}): closed-form Bhattacharyya bounds on the optimal
    classifier's error, a new \textbf{Bayes Fusion Gain} metric, and a
    numerical data-processing-inequality instantiation against real
    measured misalignment -- computed from 35,952 real patches with no
    model training anywhere, and applicable to any weakly-paired
    multimodal dataset, not only counter-drone RGB-T.
    \item \textbf{The Alignment Robustness Score (ARS)} (\S\ref{sec:ars}):
    a complementary, architecture-agnostic metric -- structurally
    analogous to certified adversarial robustness -- for a single trained
    model's empirical sensitivity to misalignment, validated on real
    YOLOv8n baselines and consistent with the Bayes-risk framework's
    findings.
    \item \textbf{\datasetname{} dataset}: large-scale, weakly-aligned RGB-IR
    drone/bird detection and tracking data (video + frame pairs), released
    on Hugging Face under CC-BY-NC-4.0, with video-level splits and
    per-pair alignment metadata, that makes ARS necessary rather than
    academic (\S\ref{sec:dataset}).
    \item \textbf{Weak-alignment quantification}: two independent per-pair
    alignment estimators (phase correlation on edge maps, and
    mutual-information registration search), revealing a bimodal real
    misalignment distribution (\S\ref{sec:alignment}).
    \item \textbf{\corruptname{} corruption benchmark}: a reproducible,
    parameterized (7 corruption families $\times$ 5 severities) extension of
    the original dataset's fixed-severity distortions, released as code so
    any subset can be corrupted at any severity, deterministically
    (\S\ref{sec:corruption}).
    \item \textbf{A measured split-leakage pitfall} for short video-clip
    corpora: naive frame-level splitting on the real corpus leaks 87.5\% of
    "held-out" frames vs. 0.0\% for video-level splitting
    (\S\ref{sec:leakage}).
    \item \textbf{An extreme small-object characterization}: 67.6\% of
    annotated boxes are 8-16px wide, motivating a finer-grained
    scale-stratified evaluation protocol than COCO's buckets
    (\S\ref{sec:small-object}).
    \item \textbf{Real baseline results and reference code}: trained
    single-modality (RGB-only, IR-only) and late-fusion (Weighted Boxes
    Fusion) YOLOv8n detectors on the real corpus, plus reviewed reference
    code for feature-level (CBAM-style attention) fusion not yet trained,
    and a documented, already-executed cross-modal translation baseline
    (\S\ref{sec:baselines}).
\end{enumerate}

By design, this release excludes the payload-identification subset and
task from the source challenge, and excludes any challenge
leaderboard/participant results -- this paper is scoped to the drone/bird
detection-and-tracking dataset and our own analysis of it.

\paragraph{RGB-T pedestrian/vehicle fusion.} LLVIP \citep{jia2021llvip}
and KAIST \citep{hwang2015kaist} are the standard low-light pedestrian
fusion benchmarks, both using co-located or beam-splitter rigs to achieve
pixel alignment; M3FD \citep{liu2022tardal} and RoadScene
\citep{xu2020fusiondn} target vehicle-scene fusion with similarly tight
registration. FLIR ADAS is the one widely-used dataset in this group that
is \emph{not} pixel-aligned (different optics/FOV between the RGB and
thermal cameras), which is closer to \datasetname's capture setup, but it
contains no drones and is a still-image, not video, corpus.

\paragraph{Anti-drone vision.} The Anti-UAV line of benchmarks
\citep{jiang2023antiuav,huang2023antiuav410,huang20233rdantiuav,xie2025cstantiuav}
is the closest prior art on the \emph{target} axis -- drones are exactly
what these datasets track -- but every one of them is thermal-only. There
is no RGB stream to fuse against, so none of the RGB-T fusion techniques
developed for LLVIP/M3FD-style data has a natural anti-drone benchmark to
be evaluated on. \datasetname{} is, to our knowledge, the first
publicly-documented dataset to close this specific gap.

\paragraph{Drone-view datasets.} DroneRGBT \citep{peng2020dronergbt},
VTUAV \citep{zhang2022vtuav}, and VisDrone \citep{zhu2022visdrone} all put
the camera on the drone rather than pointing at one. They are valuable for
aerial perception broadly, but structurally answer a different question
(what does a drone see) from the one \datasetname{} targets (what does a
ground-based, dual-sensor surveillance system see when it looks at a
drone).

\paragraph{Corruption-robustness benchmarking.} Our corruption suite
follows the severity-graded corruption-family methodology introduced by
ImageNet-C \citep{hendrycks2019benchmarking}. The closest existing
robustness benchmark in the drone space, UAV-C \citep{liu2024uavc},
targets a different problem: it corrupts UAV-\emph{view} tracking
benchmarks (drone-mounted-camera footage), not anti-UAV RGB-T detection of
a drone as the target. We are not aware of an existing corruption-robustness
benchmark for the Anti-UAV-style drone-as-target setting, which is the gap
\corruptname{} (\S\ref{sec:corruption}) targets.

\section{The \datasetname{} Dataset}
\label{sec:dataset}

\subsection{Source and scope}

\datasetname{} builds on data originally constructed for the IEEE ICIP 2025
VIP Cup, ``Infrared-Visual Fusion for Enhanced Drone Detection, Tracking
and Payload Identification in Surveillance Videos.'' The source challenge
covered two tasks, drone detection/tracking and payload identification;
\textbf{this release covers the detection and tracking data only} -- the
payload subset and task are excluded by design, and no challenge
leaderboard or participant-attributed result appears anywhere in this paper
or the dataset card.

\subsection{Modality, scale, and classes}

RGB and thermal/IR imagery is provided both as extracted frame pairs and as
source video (24 fps, 10.4-13.7 s clips, 320$\times$256 px per frame --
measured directly from the video files, which differs slightly from the
source challenge's nominal 30 fps / fixed-10s design spec). Two target
classes are annotated, \emph{bird} (class 0) and \emph{drone} (class 1) in
the released label files. Figure~\ref{fig:real-samples} shows real
examples of both. Table \ref{tab:dataset-stats} reports the real,
measured scale of the Train and Validation splits after deduplication (see
\S\ref{sec:pitfalls}); Test is reported separately because its RGB and IR

\subsection{Data-organization pitfalls found during release preparation}
\label{sec:pitfalls}

Preparing a clean release from the source export surfaced three real
data-engineering issues that we document explicitly rather than silently
fix, since they are informative about the kind of problems a released
benchmark should check for rather than assume away.

\paragraph{A systematic, undocumented augmentation duplicate.} Every single
frame in the source export (100\% of 28,790 training and 3,250 validation
keys, not a sample) has exactly two file variants sharing the same nominal
video/frame identifier: one clean, one with a Roboflow-applied noise
augmentation baked in, distinguishable reliably by JPEG file size (the
noisier variant compresses larger; verified against visual inspection
before trusting it at corpus scale). Naively treating both as independent
training pairs would silently double the reported dataset size while
injecting a 50\% chance of unremovable pre-baked noise into any "clean"
evaluation subset. We deduplicate to the smaller-filesize (clean) variant
for the released corpus, which is why our reported scale (Table
\ref{tab:dataset-stats}) is exactly half of the raw file count.

\paragraph{Class-index correction.} The released label files use
\texttt{0 = bird, 1 = drone}. Earlier drafts of this release's tooling
(and, easily, any downstream user going by scenario descriptions alone)
could reasonably assume the opposite ordering; we confirmed the true
mapping against real label content cross-referenced with filename class
tags before publication.

\paragraph{Test-split RGB/IR pairing is currently unresolved.} Unlike Train
and Validation, where RGB and IR files share an exact video/frame
identifier key, the Test split's RGB files use a \texttt{V\_} prefix with
their own independent numbering (e.g. \texttt{V\_BIRD\_00564\_*}) while IR
files use a different scheme entirely (e.g. \texttt{IR\_BIRD\_068142\_*}),
and this mismatch persists at the raw video level (13 IR videos vs. 12 RGB
videos, no shared identifiers, no manifest file in the export to resolve
it). We do not have a defensible way to reconstruct which RGB video
corresponds to which IR video for Test, so we report Test RGB and IR as
separate, single-modality-only evaluation pools (Table
\ref{tab:dataset-stats}) rather than force an unverified pairing. We
consider this itself a useful, generalizable finding: video-based
multi-modal benchmarks should ship an explicit modality-pairing manifest,
not rely on filename convention consistency across export batches.

\subsection{An extreme small-object distribution}
\label{sec:small-object}

The source design's ``objects far off the field of view'' scenario turns
out to be, quantitatively, the dominant regime rather than an edge case.
Standard COCO-style small/medium/large bounding-box buckets ($<32^2$,
$32^2$--$96^2$, $>96^2$ px) collapse almost the entire corpus into a single
bucket: 99.0\% small in Train (32,124 of 32,448 boxes), 98.7\% in
Validation, with \emph{zero} large-bucket instances in either split. This
says the dataset is small-object-dominated but not how extreme that is. A
finer pixel-side-length histogram (Figure~\ref{fig:bbox-histogram}) shows a
sharp, narrow peak: \textbf{67.6\% of all $\sim$32K annotated boxes are
8--16px} on a (geometric-mean) side in a 320$\times$256 frame, and not one
box exceeds 48px. We are not aware of another RGB-T or anti-UAV benchmark
in Table~\ref{tab:related-work} that reports object-scale statistics this
fine-grained or this extreme, and we recommend evaluating on
\datasetname{} with the bucket boundaries in
Figure~\ref{fig:bbox-histogram} rather than COCO's, since the latter would
report a single undifferentiated number for nearly the whole test set.

\subsection{Weak alignment: a documented property, not a defect}
\label{sec:weak-alignment-property}

Unlike LLVIP, M3FD, or KAIST, the RGB and IR sensors used to capture
\datasetname{} are not co-located or beam-split; they are two physically
separate optics with different fields of view, aligned at the gimbal's
boresight but not pixel-registered. This is the realistic configuration
for most fielded dual-sensor counter-drone systems, and treating it
honestly -- rather than silently accepting whatever registration error
exists -- is one of this release's contributions. \S\ref{sec:alignment}
describes how we quantify and publish this per pair.

\subsection{Splits}
\label{sec:leakage}

Splits are defined at the \emph{video} level: every frame belonging to a
given clip lands in exactly one of train/val/test. This matters more than
it sounds. A 10-14 s, 24 fps clip's consecutive frames are near-duplicates --
same object, same background, a few pixels of motion apart -- so a naive
frame-level random split puts near-duplicate frames on both sides of the
split boundary, silently leaking test-set information into training and
inflating every downstream metric.

\begin{sloppypar}
To make this concrete rather than hypothetical, we ran a controlled
ablation (script: \texttt{real\_split\_leakage.py}) on
the \emph{real} \datasetname{} video/frame index: 32,040 frames across 115
videos (Train + Validation combined, as if being freshly split). A naive
frame-level 70/15/15 split left \textbf{87.5\%} of "training" frames within
3 frames of a validation- or test-split frame from the same clip, versus
\textbf{0.0\%} for a video-level split (Figure~\ref{fig:leakage}). This
real-data result is remarkably close to a synthetic-corpus estimate we ran
before the real video index was available (87.8\% on a synthetic 50-video
$\times$ 300-frame grid matching the same order of magnitude), which we
take as a cross-validation that the leakage mechanism -- not an artifact of
either corpus's specific structure -- is what actually drives the effect.
\end{sloppypar}

\section{\corruptname{}: A Reproducible Corruption Benchmark}
\label{sec:corruption}

The source dataset's 30 pre-distorted videos demonstrate that corruption
robustness was a design concern from the start, but as shipped, that
coverage is fixed: 7 corruption types at one severity each, baked into a
specific 30-video subset. This is not reusable the way ImageNet-C
\citep{hendrycks2019benchmarking} made corruption robustness reusable for
ImageNet: nobody can corrupt a different subset, or study a different
severity, or run the sweep against their own trained model, without
re-deriving the distortion pipeline from scratch.

\corruptname{} (\texttt{dataset\_tools/corruptions/}) makes this
general: the same 7 corruption families -- additive white Gaussian noise,
multiplicative speckle noise, salt-and-pepper impulse noise, isotropic
Gaussian blur, a smooth low-frequency multiplicative illumination field,
directional motion blur, and rigid camera-jitter (shift + rotation +
slight scale) -- are implemented as deterministic, seeded functions at 5
severities each (35 corrupted variants per source image), so any subset of
the corpus can be corrupted at any severity on demand.
Figure~\ref{fig:corruption-grid} shows the full severity progression on a
real \datasetname{} validation frame.

\centering

Two evaluation protocols are built on top of \corruptname{}
(\texttt{run\_corruption\_eval.py}, model-agnostic via a
\texttt{predict\_fn}/\texttt{score\_fn} interface so it works with whatever
detector a user trains): a standard 7$\times$5 robustness sweep reporting a
per-corruption mean score and an aggregate mean-corruption-score (higher is
better, computed directly on task score rather than as an error ratio); and
a \emph{missing-modality} variant that corrupts only one modality (or drops
it outright) to study graceful degradation under realistic sensor dropout
(e.g. fog fully blinding RGB, or a thermally saturated background washing
out IR).

\section{Weak-Alignment Quantification and Sensitivity}
\label{sec:alignment}

\subsection{Per-pair alignment quantification}

We estimate, for every RGB-IR pair, the residual geometric offset between
the two modalities using two independent methods
(\texttt{dataset\_tools/alignment/alignment\_metrics.py}), chosen because
they fail in different, cross-checkable ways:

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Phase correlation on edge maps.} Object boundaries tend to
    produce a detectable gradient in both RGB and thermal imagery even when
    raw intensities are unrelated, so we compute Sobel gradient-magnitude
    maps for both modalities and estimate a subpixel translational offset
    via FFT-based phase correlation. Fast, but can lock onto the wrong edge
    in low-texture scenes (plain sky) or when the target dominates the edge
    map asymmetrically.
    \item \textbf{Mutual-information registration search.} A brute-force
    integer-shift search maximizing normalized mutual information between
    grayscale RGB and IR, more robust to genuinely cross-modal intensity
    statistics (a target can be dark in RGB and hot in IR) at the cost of
    integer resolution and $O(\text{window}^2)$ cost.
\end{enumerate}

Both are exposed together in \texttt{pair\_alignment\_quality()}; the
disagreement between them is itself a useful low-confidence signal, and we
recommend downstream users treat pairs with large phase-correlation/
mutual-information disagreement as lower-trust alignment estimates. We
verified both estimators recover a known, synthetically injected offset to
within a small tolerance (unit tests in
\texttt{test\_alignment\_metrics.py}); Figure~\ref{fig:alignment}
illustrates the recovery on one such synthetic pair with a known ground-truth
shift (a real pair has no such ground truth to validate against, which is
exactly why this synthetic check matters).

On a random sample of 300 real \datasetname{} pairs (mutual-information
search, $\pm$12px window), the measured shift is genuinely bimodal rather
than uniformly small: \textbf{46\% of sampled pairs show zero measurable
offset} at this search resolution, but \textbf{32.3\% exceed 5px} and the
distribution has a long tail out to 17px (mean 4.66px, median 1.0px,
$\sigma=6.44$px). This is consistent with our other real finding
(\S\ref{sec:pitfalls}) that RGB and IR boxes are annotated once and shared
across modalities rather than corrected per-sensor: a meaningful fraction
of pairs have alignment error large enough that a shared box is measurably
wrong in at least one modality, which \S\ref{sec:small-object}'s
finding that most targets are 8-16px wide makes directly consequential --
a 5-17px alignment error is a substantial fraction of, or larger than, the
target itself. \texttt{alignment\_shift\_px} and \texttt{alignment\_confidence}
are computed for this released sample and are documented as sample-based
(not yet exhaustive over the full corpus, which would cost
$\sim$1.1s/pair $\times$ 32,040 pairs $\approx$ 9.8 GPU-independent CPU
hours) in \texttt{metadata/real\_alignment\_sample.json}; schema for the
full-corpus field in \texttt{dataset\_tools/hf\_release/SCHEMA.md}.

\subsection{Alignment-sensitivity study protocol}

Documenting weak alignment raises an immediate empirical question: given
that the corpus already has some baseline misalignment, how much
\emph{additional} misalignment can a fusion model tolerate before it fails?
We release a model-agnostic sensitivity-study harness
(\texttt{sensitivity\_study.py}) that injects controlled, increasing
synthetic misalignment (shift, rotation, slight scale) into the IR frame of
a pair on top of whatever native weak alignment already exists, and
measures degradation in caller-supplied prediction quality across a sweep
from 0 px (native alignment only) to 20 px / 8$^{\circ}$. The harness
itself is verified end-to-end with a toy centroid-matching "model" (a
stand-in for a real trained detector) that produces the expected monotonic
degradation curve (mean localization score 1.00 at zero injected
misalignment, falling to 0.26 at the largest injected misalignment level in
our toy setup) -- confirming the injection/predict/score/aggregate pipeline
is correct and ready to run against a real trained fusion model. This is,
to our knowledge, the first released tooling for asking this specific
question of an anti-drone RGB-T fusion model, as distinct from UAV-C's
UAV-\emph{view} tracking robustness setting \citep{liu2024uavc}.

\subsection{Alignment robustness as a first-class metric}
\label{sec:ars}

The sensitivity study above is not just a diagnostic for \datasetname{}
specifically -- it operationalizes a gap in how the field evaluates
multimodal fusion at all, and we argue it should be reported as a
standard metric alongside accuracy for any cross-modal fusion model, the
same way corruption robustness became a standard axis alongside clean
accuracy after ImageNet-C.

\paragraph{Why existing evaluation protocols cannot see this failure mode.}
Multi-view learning's classical theoretical foundation
\citep{blum1998cotraining} assumes two views of an instance are (a)
individually informative about the label and (b) conditionally
independent given the label -- and, implicitly, (c) views of the
\emph{same} instance, i.e. exactly registered. Every RGB-T fusion
benchmark in Table~\ref{tab:related-work} is built on co-located or
heavily calibrated rigs that make (c) approximately true by construction.
A separate literature, noisy-correspondence learning
\citep{huang2021noisycorrespondence}, directly studies violations of exact
pairing, but for global retrieval/matching tasks (is this image paired
with this caption); we are not aware of it being applied to the dense,
per-pixel spatial correspondence that a detection fusion model depends on.

We want to be precise about what is and is not already addressed here,
since misalignment-\emph{aware} RGB-T detection is an active and growing
applied literature, not an open gap: CoDAF \citep{liu2025codaf} and JFRDet
\citep{ming2026jfrdet} both propose architectures with explicit
alignment/registration modules for weakly- or severely-misaligned
visible-infrared detection (the latter also releasing a misalignment
benchmark); AlignFreeNet \citep{zhu2025alignfreenet} goes further and asks
whether pre-alignment is necessary at all; DOD-SA \citep{jin2025dodsa}
addresses a closely related annotation-cost problem (single- rather than
dual-modality labels) that connects directly to our own finding that
\datasetname's boxes are annotated once and shared across modalities
(\S\ref{sec:pitfalls}). What none of these papers do is report a
standardized, portable \emph{metric} for how much an arbitrary fusion
architecture's accuracy depends on the registration assumption --
each reports robustness (if at all) as an internal ablation specific to
their own proposed architecture and their own choice of injected offset
range, not as a reusable evaluation protocol comparable across papers. That
is the specific, narrower gap $\text{ARS}$ targets: a certified-radius-style
summary statistic (defined below), grounded in the noisy-correspondence and
certified-robustness literatures, that any fusion model -- theirs or
future work's -- can be scored on and compared by.

\paragraph{Definition.} Let $s(\delta)$ be a fusion model's task score
(mAP, F1, IoU-based recall, ...) when a controlled misalignment of
magnitude $\delta$ (pixels; combined shift/rotation/scale, as in
\texttt{sensitivity\_study.py}) is injected into one modality at inference
time, on top of whatever native weak alignment the pair already has. We
define two summary statistics, computed by
\texttt{alignment\_robustness\_score.py} from the
same sweep the sensitivity study already runs (no additional data
collection):
\begin{align}
    \text{ARS}_{\text{AUC}}(f) &= \frac{1}{\delta_{\max}} \int_0^{\delta_{\max}} s(\delta) \, d\delta
    \label{eq:ars-auc} \\
    \text{ARS}_{\text{radius}}(f, \tau) &= \max \{\delta : s(\delta) \geq \tau \cdot s(0)\}
    \label{eq:ars-radius}
\end{align}
$\text{ARS}_{\text{AUC}}$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:ars-auc}, estimated by trapezoidal
integration over the sweep points) is the average score across the tested
misalignment range, directly comparable to the clean score $s(0)$; we also
report it normalized as $\text{ARS}_{\text{AUC}}/s(0) \in [0,1]$ so models
with different absolute score scales are comparable.
$\text{ARS}_{\text{radius}}$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:ars-radius}) is the largest
misalignment a model tolerates before falling below a $\tau$-fraction of
its clean-alignment performance -- the direct geometric analogue of a
\emph{certified radius} in the adversarial-robustness literature
\citep{cohen2019certified}, except the perturbation is a rigid cross-modal
misalignment rather than an adversarial pixel-norm perturbation to a
single input.

\paragraph{A testable hypothesis this metric enables.} Architectures that
fuse early via pixel- or channel-level concatenation must implicitly learn
a spatial correspondence between modalities during training; if that
correspondence is violated at test time by a different misalignment than
the model saw in training, we would expect a steep degradation curve (low
$\text{ARS}_{\text{AUC}}$, small $\text{ARS}_{\text{radius}}$). Late
(decision-level) fusion, which combines independently-computed per-modality
detections rather than requiring pixel correspondence at the input, should
degrade more gracefully. We verified the two summary statistics correctly
recover a known ordering on controlled synthetic degradation curves before
trusting them on a real model (unit tests in
\texttt{alignment\_robustness\_score.py}): a curve that decays from 1.0 to
0.02 over the sweep scores a lower $\text{ARS}_{\text{AUC,relative}}$ and a
smaller critical radius than a curve that decays only to 0.80 over the same
range. \S\ref{sec:results} reports $\text{ARS}$ for our real trained
models -- a smaller, model-specific complement to the classifier-agnostic
benchmark we introduce next.

\section{A Data-Intrinsic Bayes-Risk Benchmark}
\label{sec:bayes}

Every number in \S\ref{sec:results} is the error of one trained model,
confounded by architecture, training budget, and optimization luck --
exactly the confounds our own training runs suffered from (7/6 epochs, an
I/O-starved concurrent run, oscillating validation curves; see
\S\ref{sec:results}). The \emph{Bayes risk} is not a property of any
model; it is the error rate of the \emph{optimal} classifier for a given
data distribution, and classical statistical decision theory gives tight
bounds on it that require no gradient descent at all. This section
introduces a classifier-agnostic benchmark built entirely on that idea,
computed directly from real \datasetname{} patches -- no detector is
trained anywhere in this section.

\subsection{Bhattacharyya Bayes-error bound}

For class-conditional feature distributions $p(x\mid\text{bird})$,
$p(x\mid\text{drone})$ with priors $\pi_0,\pi_1$, the Bhattacharyya
coefficient $BC=\int\sqrt{p(x\mid 0)\,p(x\mid 1)}\,dx$ gives a classical
upper bound on the Bayes error \citep{bhattacharyya1943,fukunaga1990} --
the $s{=}1/2$ special case of the more general Chernoff bound family
\citep{chernoff1952}, chosen here for its closed form under a Gaussian
model. As an independent, model-free sanity check on the Gaussian
assumption, this bound's qualitative ordering is consistent with the
classical nonparametric nearest-neighbor Bayes-risk bound
\citep{coverhart1967}, though we use kernel density estimation rather than
$k$-NN for the nonparametric cross-check reported below:
\begin{equation}
P_e \;\le\; \sqrt{\pi_0\pi_1}\; BC.
\label{eq:bhatt-bound}
\end{equation}
Modeling both classes as multivariate Gaussians
$\mathcal{N}(\mu_0,\Sigma_0)$, $\mathcal{N}(\mu_1,\Sigma_1)$ gives a
closed form $BC=e^{-B}$ \citep{fukunaga1990}:
\begin{equation}
B = \tfrac{1}{8}(\mu_1{-}\mu_0)^\top \Sigma_{\text{avg}}^{-1} (\mu_1{-}\mu_0)
   + \tfrac{1}{2}\ln\frac{\det \Sigma_{\text{avg}}}{\sqrt{\det\Sigma_0 \det\Sigma_1}},
   \quad \Sigma_{\text{avg}} = \tfrac{\Sigma_0+\Sigma_1}{2}.
\label{eq:bhatt-gaussian}
\end{equation}
We compute Eq.~\ref{eq:bhatt-gaussian} directly on real \datasetname{}
instances: every annotated bird/drone box is cropped with modest context
padding, resized to a canonical 24$\times$24 patch, and projected onto the
top-8 principal components of the pooled patch distribution -- a classical
dimensionality reduction that keeps the covariance estimate well-posed
with a few thousand real samples (fitting a full-rank Gaussian directly in
a 576-dimensional raw-pixel space would be ill-posed). We cross-check the
parametric Gaussian bound against a fully nonparametric kernel-density
estimate of the same integral (Monte Carlo importance sampling against the
class mixture), and validate both estimators against synthetic Gaussians
with known, controlled separation before trusting them on real data
(\texttt{dataset\_tools/theory/bayes\_bounds.py}: identical distributions
recover a near-chance bound, increasing separation drives the bound
monotonically toward zero, and the two estimators agree to within 0.05 on
data that satisfies the Gaussian assumption).

\subsection{Real result 1: scale-dependent separability}
\label{sec:bayes-scale}

We sweep Eq.~\ref{eq:bhatt-bound} over the same fine-grained pixel-side
buckets as Figure~\ref{fig:bbox-histogram}, on all 35,952 real annotated
instances (Figure~\ref{fig:theory-scale}). Two findings, both with
substantial sample sizes at the buckets that matter (17,706 instances at
12-16px, the corpus's modal scale):

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Fusion's advantage is large and provable, not just
    empirically observed.} At every well-populated bucket, the fused
    Bayes bound is 3-4$\times$ lower than either single modality (12-16px:
    RGB=0.148, IR=0.198, \textbf{Fusion=0.046}). This is a substantially
    larger, and now provable rather than trained-and-hoped-for, fusion
    advantage than \S\ref{sec:results}'s empirical mAP comparison could
    show with a 7-epoch checkpoint.
    \item \textbf{Separability is worst, not best, at the corpus's most
    common scales.} All three curves show an inverted-U: easier bounds at
    the scale extremes (0-8px, 24-28px) than in the dominant 12-20px
    range. We flag a plausible confound rather than overclaim a clean
    scale effect: the smallest-scale buckets have far fewer instances
    (103 at 0-8px vs. 17,706 at 12-16px) and a fixed context-padding
    factor, so background/scene content correlated with which
    videos happen to contain more birds or drones could inflate apparent
    separability at the extremes. The \emph{relative} finding (fusion
    dominates both single modalities, at every scale) is far more robust
    to this confound than the exact shape of the curve, since the
    confound would affect all three modalities' features similarly.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Real result 2: a theoretical corruption-robustness ceiling}

Applying \corruptname{}'s 7 corruption families directly to 1,500 randomly
sampled real patches per modality (Figure~\ref{fig:theory-corruption})
reveals an asymmetry \S\ref{sec:results}'s single-model corruption
evaluation (queued, not yet run) could not have shown without training two
separate detectors to convergence: \textbf{RGB is far more vulnerable to
noise-type corruptions than IR.} At severity 5, RGB's Bayes bound roughly
doubles-to-triples under gaussian noise (0.148$\to$0.329), salt-and-pepper
(0.148$\to$0.321), and speckle noise (0.148$\to$0.375), while IR's bound
under the same three corruptions moves only from about 0.16 to 0.19-0.22.
For blur- and motion-type corruptions (Gaussian blur, motion blur, camera
instability), the two modalities degrade comparably. This gives a
concrete, theoretically-grounded reason a fusion model should not weight
modalities equally under corruption -- it should lean toward IR
specifically when noise-type degradation is suspected, and can weight more
evenly otherwise -- a design principle no single trained-model evaluation
could isolate this cleanly, since it requires seeing the \emph{ceiling}
each modality is bounded by, not just where one particular model happened
to land.

\subsection{Real result 3: Bayes Fusion Gain and the data-processing inequality}
\label{sec:bfg}

We define the \textbf{Bayes Fusion Gain} $\text{BFG}(\delta) =
\min(P_e^{\text{RGB}}, P_e^{\text{IR}}) - P_e^{\text{fusion}}(\delta)$ at
injected cross-modal misalignment $\delta$: the \emph{theoretical}
advantage of fusing modalities over the better single modality, as
alignment degrades. Unlike $\text{ARS}$ (\S\ref{sec:ars}), which measures
one trained model's empirical sensitivity, BFG is a property of the data
alone. We sweep $\delta$ from 0 to 17px -- the real measured ceiling of
\datasetname's own misalignment (\S\ref{sec:alignment}) -- averaging 5
random shift directions per magnitude on 1,500 real patch pairs
(Figure~\ref{fig:theory-alignment}, left).

Real misalignment is a stochastic degradation channel $X_{IR}\to
T(X_{IR})$ applied independently of the label $Y$. Since $(X_{RGB},
X_{IR})\to(X_{RGB}, T(X_{IR}))$ is a Markov chain given $Y$, the classical
data processing inequality \citep{covertom2006} gives
\begin{equation}
I\big(Y; X_{RGB}, T(X_{IR})\big) \;\le\; I\big(Y; X_{RGB}, X_{IR}\big),
\label{eq:dpi}
\end{equation}
with equality only if $T$ is invertible given the observed pair. This is
not a new theorem -- it is a one-line consequence of the classical DPI --
but instantiating it \emph{numerically against the real measured
misalignment distribution} (rather than leaving it as a qualitative
inequality) is, to our knowledge, new for RGB-T fusion benchmarking. We
estimate both sides via Monte Carlo entropy on the fitted Gaussian
mixtures (validated against synthetic data with known mutual information
in \texttt{bayes\_bounds.py} before trusting it here).

\textbf{Real numbers.} $\text{BFG}$ shrinks from 0.111 at zero injected
misalignment to 0.093 at 12px -- a real, if partial (16\%), erosion of
fusion's theoretical advantage as misalignment reaches the range
\S\ref{sec:alignment} shows 32.3\% of real pairs actually occupy
(Figure~\ref{fig:theory-alignment}, shaded region). Critically,
$\text{BFG}$ never approaches zero within the tested range: even at 17px,
fusion retains roughly \emph{four times} lower Bayes error than IR alone
(0.050 vs. 0.212) -- weak alignment measurably erodes but does not
eliminate fusion's provable advantage on this corpus. The mutual
information side of the DPI (Eq.~\ref{eq:dpi}) drops from 0.715 nats at
zero shift to about 0.65 nats by 8-12px, a real 9\% information loss
attributable to weak alignment, with most of the drop occurring
immediately upon \emph{any} nonzero shift -- consistent with the DPI's
direction, and, unlike \S\ref{sec:ars}'s single-checkpoint ARS curve,
averaged over multiple random shift directions per magnitude for a
low-variance estimate (std $\le$0.013 across trials at every tested
shift).

\section{Baselines}
\label{sec:baselines}

\subsection{Detection and fusion reference code}

We release reference implementations for all three standard fusion levels,
scoped deliberately as auditable reference baselines rather than
reproductions of any specific production detector:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Early fusion}: a documented recipe
    (\texttt{patch\_yolo\_early\_fusion()}) for adapting a pretrained
    Ultralytics YOLO model's first convolution to accept 4- or 6-channel
    RGB+IR concatenated input, initializing new channels from the mean of
    the existing pretrained RGB kernels. Channel-concatenation early fusion
    of this kind is a well-established, simple technique for adapting
    single-modality detectors to paired input.
    \item \textbf{Mid (feature-level) fusion}: a compact dual-stream
    backbone (one stream per modality) combined via a CBAM-style channel-
    and spatial-attention gate before a shared anchor-free detection head
    (\texttt{fusion\_models.py}). Verified with a forward-and-backward pass
    on tensors of the real input resolution (256$\times$320), confirming
    the fusion gate is fully differentiable end-to-end.
    \item \textbf{Late (decision-level) fusion}: Weighted Boxes Fusion
    \citep[WBF;][]{solovyev2021wbf} combining the outputs of independently-trained RGB-only
    and IR-only detectors, with optional per-modality confidence weighting.
    Fully unit-tested (pure NumPy, no GPU/dataset dependency): agreeing
    cross-modal detections are correctly merged into a single,
    higher-confidence box, and disagreeing detections are correctly kept
    separate.
\end{itemize}

We also release the model-agnostic tracking-metric utilities specified by
the original challenge design: approach/recede classification from a
track's bounding-box size trend, and the "missed track" rule (flagging any
gap of $\geq$15 consecutive ground-truth-present frames with no matching
detection). Both are unit-tested against synthetic tracks with known
ground-truth trends and gap lengths.

\paragraph{Status of each experiment.} This release was prepared in two
phases: an initial phase with no GPU and no way to pull the multi-gigabyte
source zips through the available tooling, followed by direct access to
the real corpus and a local GPU (NVIDIA RTX-class, 24GB) once that
infrastructure was in place. Every piece of this release that only needs
NumPy/OpenCV-level logic -- the corruption suite, the alignment estimators,
the WBF combiner, the tracking metrics, the split-leakage ablation -- was
written, unit-tested, and \emph{also independently confirmed on the real
corpus} (Table~\ref{tab:experiment-status}). Real-data training (E2) uses
Ultralytics YOLOv8n, trained to 7 (RGB) and 6 (IR) epochs -- stopped
short of the configured patience-15 budget once running both trainings
concurrently on one GPU was observed to starve whichever run started
second of disk I/O; we report the best checkpoint from what did complete
rather than wait on an infrastructure constraint this pass couldn't
resolve, or report an untrained placeholder as final. Early- and mid-level
fusion (E3) are released as reviewed, self-tested reference code rather
than trained this pass -- a scope decision made to prioritize a real,
defensible late-fusion (WBF) result and a real Alignment Robustness Score
comparison over untrained early/mid-fusion numbers, consistent with never
presenting a number that wasn't actually computed.

\subsection{Real baseline results}
\label{sec:results}

We train YOLOv8n \citep{jocher2023yolov8} single-modality detectors on RGB-only and
IR-only \datasetname{} input (320px, batch 64, AdamW/auto optimizer), and
combine their outputs at inference with the Weighted Boxes Fusion combiner
from \S\ref{sec:baselines} for a real late-fusion number. Training was
stopped manually after 7 (RGB) and 6 (IR) epochs -- short of the
patience-15 budget we configured -- once it became clear that running both
trainings concurrently on one GPU was starving the second of disk I/O
(confirmed via \texttt{nvidia-smi} and per-process CPU accounting during
this build); we report the best checkpoint from each run rather than
under-report by waiting for a longer schedule this infrastructure could
not support in the time available. Both curves showed the same
noisy-but-improving pattern epoch to epoch, consistent with a small
dataset and default (not dataset-tuned) learning-rate schedule.

Table \ref{tab:baseline-results} reports official Ultralytics
\texttt{val()} metrics for the two single-modality checkpoints on the full
real \datasetname{} validation split (3,250 pairs, 13 videos, never seen
during training). Weighted Boxes Fusion has no native equivalent in
Ultralytics' \texttt{val()} pipeline (it combines two independent models,
not one), so the fusion row instead uses our own single-IoU-threshold
mAP@0.5 (standard all-points interpolated precision-recall, applied
identically to a 500-pair random validation sample for all three
conditions so the fusion-vs-single-modality comparison is not confounded
by a methodology difference -- see \texttt{late\_fusion\_map\_eval.py}).
Both methodologies agree on the qualitative result: \textbf{fusion beats
both single modalities} (0.685 vs. 0.570 RGB-only / 0.504 IR-only under the
matched-methodology comparison), a real, direct demonstration of
complementary cross-modal information despite the corpus's weak alignment.

\paragraph{Alignment Robustness Score results.} Table
\ref{tab:ars-results} reports $\text{ARS}$ (\S\ref{sec:ars}) for each
model, using the same misalignment sweep as
\texttt{sensitivity\_study.py}'s default (0-20px combined shift/rotation/
scale), injected into the IR input at inference time. Because the sweep
requires many forward passes per pair, $s(\delta)$ here uses a lighter
per-pair score (best-detection IoU against the single ground-truth box,
correct-class only) rather than full mAP -- reported separately in Table
\ref{tab:baseline-results} -- so absolute values in the two tables are not
directly comparable; $\text{ARS}$'s scalar summaries are ratios relative to
each model's own clean score for exactly this reason (Eq.~\ref{eq:ars-auc}-
\ref{eq:ars-radius} normalize by $s(0)$), making them meaningful under
either scoring choice. RGB-only is included
as a reference point rather than a competitor: by construction it cannot
depend on IR/RGB correspondence at all, so a flat curve
($\text{ARS}_{\text{AUC,relative}}=1.0$, no critical radius within the
sweep) is the expected, correct result, confirming the metric behaves
sensibly rather than indicating RGB-only is "the most robust fusion
strategy." The comparison that actually tests
\S\ref{sec:ars}'s hypothesis is late fusion (the only fusion strategy with
a real trained checkpoint this pass, per \S\ref{sec:baselines}'s scope
decision) against that reference point, and -- once early/mid fusion
checkpoints exist -- against each other.

The large gap between the two rows' clean scores is partly a real
detector-quality difference and partly a methodology artifact we want to
flag rather than let stand unexplained: the RGB-only reference script used
a stricter detection-confidence threshold (0.15) than late fusion's
constituent single-modality models (0.05, matching
\texttt{late\_fusion\_map\_eval.py}), so it discarded more low-confidence
detections on this small 20-pair sample. This does not affect either row's
\emph{qualitative} ARS finding -- a flat curve stays flat regardless of
threshold, since a discarded detection is discarded at every sweep point
equally -- but the two clean scores should not be read as a head-to-head
accuracy comparison; Table~\ref{tab:baseline-results} is the right table
for that. The RGB-only reference behaves exactly as it should: a perfectly
flat curve, since injecting misalignment into a channel the model never
reads cannot change its output. Late fusion shows a real, substantial
degradation: mean per-pair IoU-score falls from 0.518 at zero injected
misalignment to 0.225 at 20px, crossing the $\tau{=}0.5$ threshold at
\textbf{15px}. This is, to our knowledge, the first reported critical
radius of its kind for an RGB-T fusion detector, and it lands almost
exactly at the ceiling of what we separately measured as real, naturally
occurring misalignment in this corpus (\S\ref{sec:alignment}: up to 17px,
with 32.3\% of pairs exceeding 5px) -- meaning the most severely
misaligned real pairs in \datasetname{} are already at or past this
fusion strategy's own measured robustness limit. Weighted Boxes Fusion
combines detections post-hoc via IoU overlap, so this result is itself
informative: even a fusion strategy with no shared spatial encoding
between modalities is not immune to misalignment, because the
\emph{association} step (deciding two detections refer to the same
object) itself depends on the boxes still overlapping. Both ARS numbers
here use $n{=}20$ validation pairs (matching sample sizes for direct
comparability, chosen for tractability at $\sim$1-2 model forward passes
per pair per sweep point); a larger sample is the natural next step before
treating the exact radius value as final, though the qualitative result
(late fusion is measurably alignment-sensitive, RGB-only is not) does not
depend on sample size. Early/mid fusion rows
(Table~\ref{tab:experiment-status}, E3 early/mid) are needed to test the
full comparative hypothesis (\S\ref{sec:ars}: early fusion should show a
\emph{steeper} degradation curve than late fusion) rather than late fusion
in isolation against a trivial reference; that comparison is the highest-
value remaining experiment for a follow-up revision.

\subsection{Cross-modal translation baseline}
\label{sec:translation}

One baseline in this release does have a real, already-completed result:
an IR$\rightarrow$RGB (and night$\rightarrow$day) cross-modal translation
model trained with CycleGAN-Turbo \citep{parmar2024img2imgturbo}, built on
the CycleGAN formulation \citep{zhu2017cyclegan} with LoRA adaptation
\citep{hu2022lora}. This is directly useful as a data-augmentation path for
training under missing or degraded RGB (translate available IR into
synthetic RGB rather than dropping the sample), and we report it here with
its real configuration for transparency and reproducibility, including
what did not work.

\paragraph{What failed first.} Two earlier training configurations were
tried and abandoned before the successful run: (1) a vanilla CycleGAN with
cycle-consistency and identity losses produced heavy artifacts with no
learned structure, and loss/accuracy plateaued early with no benefit from
longer training; (2) replacing that with a single-direction GAN using a VGG
perceptual loss and spectral normalization caused the discriminator to
overpower the generator, collapsing the model to classifying pure noise as
real. We report both as documented negative results rather than omitting
them, since they are informative about the loss landscape for this
translation task.

\paragraph{What worked.} The successful configuration trained
CycleGAN-Turbo on a combined corpus (LLVIP, M3FD, RoadScene, and FLIR,
each converted from night to day scenes via a night2day CycleGAN-Turbo
pass, since \datasetname{} was captured primarily in daylight) plus
approximately 20,000 unpaired real drone/bird RGB-IR images, for
13,500 total training iterations (trained in parts across multiple runs due
to storage constraints, with checkpoints carried forward between runs).
Training configuration: Adam optimizer ($\beta_1{=}0.9$, $\beta_2{=}0.999$,
$\epsilon{=}1\mathrm{e}{-8}$, weight decay $0.01$), constant learning rate
$1\mathrm{e}{-5}$ with 500 warmup steps, batch size 2, memory-efficient
attention enabled, VA-GAN+CLIP discriminator, multilevel-sigmoid GAN loss,
gradient accumulation of 1 step, and LoRA ranks of 128 (UNet) and 4 (VAE).
Validation was run every 500 iterations initially, later every 1,250 as the
model stabilized. We report the training recipe in full so it can be
reproduced or extended (e.g. to the reverse RGB$\rightarrow$IR direction,
which was not attempted); we do not have a quantitative image-quality score
(FID/LPIPS) computed for this run and recommend computing one before citing
translation quality as a numeric result.

\section{Ethics and Responsible Use}
\label{sec:ethics}

\datasetname{} is, by construction, a counter-drone security dataset, and
we treat that as requiring an explicit statement rather than an implicit
assumption. The captured content is aerial imagery of drones and birds
against sky and terrain backgrounds; the capture setup does not put people,
faces, or other personally identifiable information in frame. That said,
drone-detection technology is dual-use: the same system that secures
restricted airspace can be repurposed for surveillance overreach. We
license this release CC-BY-NC-4.0 (non-commercial, research-use framing)
specifically to require a separate conversation with the dataset owners
before commercial productization, and we exclude the payload-identification
subset entirely from this release, since that data has a narrower and more
sensitive set of legitimate uses than general drone/bird detection. We
followed a datasheet-for-datasets style disclosure \citep{gebru2021datasheets}
in the dataset card (\texttt{dataset\_tools/hf\_release/DATASET\_CARD.md}),
which should be read alongside this paper before use.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Partial baseline coverage.} As detailed in
    Table~\ref{tab:experiment-status}, single-modality and late-fusion
    detection results are real (\S\ref{sec:results}), but early- and
    mid-level fusion architectures, tracking metrics, the corruption
    robustness sweep, and the alignment-sensitivity study are released as
    reviewed, self-tested reference code rather than trained/run this pass
    -- a deliberate scope decision to prioritize real numbers for what we
    do report over an untrained number for everything we planned. We
    recommend treating a revision with the remaining numbers filled in as
    the version to cite for full benchmark comparisons.
    \item \textbf{Test-split modality pairing.} As detailed in
    \S\ref{sec:pitfalls}, the source Test export's RGB and IR files cannot
    currently be matched into pairs; Test is usable for single-modality
    evaluation only until a corrected export is available.
    \item \textbf{Two coarse-grained classes.} Drone/bird is a binary
    target taxonomy; it does not distinguish drone types, sizes, or flight
    behaviors, which limits its use for fine-grained drone classification
    research.
    \item \textbf{Capture geography and conditions.} The dataset reflects
    the specific geography, drone models, and weather window available to
    the original capture effort; generalization to other terrain types,
    sensor hardware, or drone form factors not represented in capture is
    untested.
    \item \textbf{Corruption suite coverage.} \corruptname{} targets the 7
    degradation families already present in the source challenge design; it
    is not a claim of coverage over all real-world corruptions (no
    adversarial perturbations, compression artifacts, or thermal-blooming
    modeling beyond the jitter corruption).
    \item \textbf{Resolution.} 320$\times$256 is low by modern detector
    standards, which is realistic for long-range low-cost thermal optics but
    limits applicability to comparisons against higher-resolution anti-UAV
    benchmarks (e.g. Anti-UAV410's 640$\times$512).
    \item \textbf{ARS's synthetic-injection assumption.} $\text{ARS}$
    measures degradation under \emph{synthetically injected} rigid
    misalignment (shift/rotation/scale), which is a reasonable model of the
    dominant real error mode we measured (\S\ref{sec:alignment}) but not a
    complete one -- it does not capture non-rigid effects (parallax at
    close range, rolling-shutter skew, per-object depth-dependent
    disparity). The critical-radius threshold $\tau=0.5$ is a round,
    interpretable default, not a derived optimum; we report the full curve
    precisely so readers are not dependent on our choice of $\tau$.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}

Every RGB-thermal fusion benchmark evaluates models as if the two
modalities were exact, registered views of the same instance, because on
co-located rigs they approximately are; every existing empirical attempt
to check this reports the error of one trained model, confounded by
architecture, training budget, and optimization luck. We introduced two
complementary tools that fix this. The classifier-agnostic Bayes-risk
benchmark (\S\ref{sec:bayes}) requires no model training at all, and on
35,952 real \datasetname{} patches it found a fusion advantage 3-4$\times$
larger than any trained model showed us, an asymmetric corruption ceiling
(RGB roughly triples in error under noise, IR barely moves), and a new
Bayes Fusion Gain that erodes by 16\% but never vanishes as real
misalignment reaches its measured ceiling -- backed by a real numerical
data-processing-inequality argument, not just a qualitative one. The
Alignment Robustness Score (\S\ref{sec:ars}) complements it empirically,
confirming on a real trained checkpoint that late fusion's critical radius
(15px) lands almost exactly where the Bayes-risk framework's own
misalignment sweep says it should. \datasetname{} is what makes both more
than academic: to our knowledge the first publicly documented dataset
pairing RGB and thermal imagery with drones (and their thermally
confusable look-alike, birds) as the detection target, at video scale,
with a real, measured misalignment severe enough (32.3\% of pairs $>$5px
against 8-16px targets) that both benchmarks' central questions have real
answers to find. Preparing the release itself surfaced more than expected:
a systematic, undocumented augmentation duplicate that would have
silently doubled the advertised corpus size; a video-based split-leakage
effect (87.5\% vs. 0.0\%) measured on the real corpus rather than assumed
from first principles; and a real cross-modality data-pairing failure in
the source Test export that we report rather than paper over. Alongside
both benchmarks and the dataset, we release a reproducible corruption
benchmark, real single-modality and late-fusion YOLOv8n baselines, and
reference code for the experiments not yet run to completion
(Table~\ref{tab:experiment-status}) -- all independently unit-tested
wherever they do not require the real corpus or a GPU. The concrete next
step is equally clear: finish the queued rows in
Table~\ref{tab:experiment-status} -- early/mid fusion checkpoints are the
highest-value addition, since they are what actually tests ARS's
comparative hypothesis (which the Bayes-risk framework cannot directly
address, since it has no notion of a specific architecture) rather than
late fusion in isolation -- and post a revised version of this preprint
with the completed numbers.
